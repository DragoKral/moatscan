This week's picks again focus on small, overlooked companies: under $3B in market value, followed by few Wall Street analysts, beaten down from their highs, and still profitable at their core. The idea is to be contrarian where it's justified. Small stocks swing harder and can be thinly traded, so keep positions small and use limit orders. Nobody can reliably predict which stocks rise in the next few weeks; these are the best odds over a year or more, not promises. Changes from last week: the same five names stay, but the order shifts. Build-A-Bear jumped about 15% after famous investor Michael Burry praised it publicly, so there is less bargain left and it moves down to fourth. Travelzoo kept its promise to grow revenue again. This week is busy: Ruger and Innoviva both report results on Wednesday, Nov 4, and Heritage Global (on the radar) reports Nov 5. New this week: each pick now explains why the stock sells so low, lists pros and cons, and gets a stress test. "If it goes badly" is the value if the market's worry comes true, and the odds compare the possible gain with the possible loss. That test moves Smith & Wesson up to second place and shows that Ruger, Build-A-Bear and Travelzoo offer poor odds at today's prices.

1. Innoviva (INVA) · ~$21, estimated value ~$49, if no growth ~$27, if it goes badly ~$12

A $1.5B royalty-and-drugs company that only five analysts follow. Even if it never grows, the cash it earns is worth more than today's price.

Latest news: Quiet week ahead of results; the stock has been flat around $21. In Q2, revenue rose 19% to $120M and hospital drug sales rose 46%. The reported loss came from its stock holdings in other companies falling in value, not from the business itself.

Why it sells so low: About half its money comes from royalties on GSK lung inhalers, and the patents behind them are expected to run out around 2031. Investors usually mark stocks like this down years ahead, and Goldman Sachs rates it a Sell for that reason. Its profits also look messy because it owns stakes in other drug companies, and its mix of royalties, drugs and investments is hard to put in a box. If the royalties fade and the new drugs don't fill the gap, it could be worth less than even the no-growth figure above.

How they plan to grow: It uses steady royalties from GSK lung inhalers (about $60M a quarter) to build a hospital-drugs business, with at least $150M of US sales expected this year, plus a newly approved pill for drug-resistant gonorrhea. For Q3, analysts expect about $115M of revenue and $0.52 of profit per share; their average price target is about $39.

Pros: Plenty of cash coming in today. Hospital drug sales are growing fast (up 46%), and analysts' average target is about $39.

Cons: A known end date for its biggest income source. Its future depends on hospital drugs, which are a tough market to sell into, and its reported profits will keep jumping around.

Is it worth it? If the royalties fade as feared and nothing replaces them, the stress test puts it at about $12, a real drop from $21. But the possible gain (to about $49) is about $3 for every $1 you could lose. Good odds. The odds stay good below about $25.

What to watch: Q3 results are expected after the market closes on Wednesday, Nov 4. Look at hospital drug sales and early sales of the new pill. Risk: the royalties fade as the inhalers age, and swings in its investments keep making profits look messy.

2. Smith & Wesson (SWBI) · ~$13, estimated value ~$25, if no growth ~$19, if it goes badly ~$8.5

A $590M gun maker that only two analysts follow. It's down about 25% from its high, yet even with no growth it looks worth about 40% more than today's price.

Latest news: No new headlines; the stock drifted down about 6% this week, which makes it cheaper. In its latest quarter (reported Sept 3), sales jumped 32% to $113M and it swung back to a small profit, helped partly by one-time tariff refunds.

Why it sells so low: It's caught in the same slump in gun demand as Ruger. Its profits are thin, and part of last quarter's improvement came from one-time tariff refunds, so investors doubt the recovery will last.

How they plan to grow: New products (38% of sales last year), plus faster-growing areas like gun silencers, hunting rifles, police and overseas sales. Management expects this quarter's sales to be about 10% above last year and full-year growth of 5–7%.

Pros: Cheap even if it never grows, sales are rising again, and new products like silencers and hunting rifles are selling well.

Cons: Profits are small and easily wiped out. The recent boost was partly one-time. Owning it alongside Ruger is a double bet on the same industry.

Is it worth it? If gun sales slump again the way they did after the pandemic boom, it's worth about $8.50. The possible gain (to about $25) is about $2.50 for every $1 at risk. Good odds, as long as you buy below about $14.

What to watch: Ruger's results on Nov 4 will hint at how gun demand is doing; Smith & Wesson's own results come in early December. Risk: profits are still thin, and owning both gun makers doubles your bet on the same industry.

3. Sturm, Ruger (RGR) · ~$44, estimated value ~$55, if no growth ~$54, if it goes badly ~$29

A debt-free US gun maker with a long record of profits, followed by only two analysts. Its CEO and directors bought shares with their own money this year, and it now ranks first on this week's small-company list.

Latest news: A special situation. Italian gunmaker Beretta now owns about 25% of Ruger after buying shares at $44.80 in a public offer that closed Oct 15. The stock has slipped just below that price, to about $44.

Why it sells so low: Gun sales have been falling since the pandemic buying spree, so profits are well below their peak. Now that Beretta's offer is finished, nothing props up the price. If gun demand keeps shrinking, the estimated value is too high.

How they plan to grow: New products and cost cuts while gun demand stays well below its pandemic peak. With a 25% owner that knows the industry, a full takeover later is possible, but nothing has been offered.

Pros: No debt, a long record of profits, and insiders buying with their own money. Beretta, a 25% owner, could make a full offer someday.

Cons: Sales are still sliding. Gun demand rises and falls with politics and headlines. Beretta might never bid.

Is it worth it? If gun sales slump again, it's worth about $29. The possible gain (to about $55) is only about $0.70 for every $1 at risk. Poor odds on the numbers alone. The main reason to hold it is the chance that Beretta makes a full offer. The odds turn good below about $37.

What to watch: Q3 results on Wednesday, Nov 4, and any next move by Beretta, such as board seats or a full bid. Risk: without the offer to lean on, the stock depends on gun sales, which are still sliding.

4. Build-A-Bear Workshop (BBW) · ~$28, estimated value ~$34, if no growth ~$33, if it goes badly ~$19

Still a good business at a fair-to-cheap price: it expects $60–68M of pre-tax profit this year on a market value of about $340M, and only three analysts cover it. It's still down more than half from its high.

Latest news: The stock had its best week in over a year, up about 15%, after investor Michael Burry wrote on Oct 29 that it has a "physical moat" Wall Street doesn't understand and valued it at about seven times this year's profit. The worry that sank it is unchanged: in late August it cut its 2026 sales forecast to $500–525M after online sales fell 16%.

Why it sells so low: Investors see it as a mall store in a world moving online. In August it cut this year's sales forecast, and online sales fell 16%, so many worry its post-pandemic boom is over and families are cutting back on treats.

How they plan to grow: More stores and partner-run locations abroad, wholesale deals that put its products in other retailers, and big "experience" stores like its new three-story flagship at ICON Park in Orlando. It keeps paying a dividend and buying back shares.

Pros: Still solidly profitable with little debt, it pays a dividend and buys back shares, and its stores offer an experience that's hard to copy online.

Cons: Sales are shrinking this year, online keeps weakening, and a weak holiday season would hurt. After this week's jump, much less of the bargain is left.

Is it worth it? If the sales slide continues, it's worth about $19. After this week's jump, the possible gain (to about $34) is only about $0.70 for every $1 at risk. Poor odds right now. They turn good again below about $24, so consider waiting for a pullback.

What to watch: The Orlando flagship opens in November, Q3 results come in early December, then the holiday season. Risk: after the jump, part of the bargain is gone, and kids' spending could stay weak.

5. Travelzoo (TZOO) · ~$5, estimated value ~$6 (assumes no growth), if it goes badly ~$2.5

A $53M travel-deals company whose founder's family owns about 36%. The stock is down about 58% since it switched to paid memberships, which cost money now but should bring steady fees later.

Latest news: It reported Q3 on Oct 27 and kept its promise: revenue grew 3% to $22.9M after falling last quarter. Paying members are up 65% from a year ago, and fees collected in advance reached $13.5M. It still lost $2M because it spends heavily on marketing to sign up members.

Why it sells so low: It's losing money while it switches to paid memberships, and it's so tiny ($53M) that most funds can't or won't buy it. Travel has also been hurt by conflicts around the world. The estimated value assumes the membership plan works; if it doesn't, the stock is worth less.

How they plan to grow: Building recurring membership fees on top of advertising. Management expects revenue to keep growing in Q4 and beyond, with profits improving over time. It has also been buying back its own shares ($5.7M so far this year).

Pros: Revenue is growing again, paying members are up 65%, the founder's family owns about 36%, and the company keeps buying back its own shares.

Cons: Still losing money with only about $9M of cash. Few shares trade each day, so prices can jump. The membership plan isn't proven yet.

Is it worth it? If the membership switch fails, it's worth about $2.50, less than half today's price. The possible gain (to about $6.30) is only about $0.40 for every $1 at risk. Poor odds. Keep it very small or skip it. The odds turn good only below about $3.75.

What to watch: Q4 results in February will show whether growth picks up as membership fees build. This is the highest-risk pick: it's tiny, still losing money, and has only about $9M of cash.
