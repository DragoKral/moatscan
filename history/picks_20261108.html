This week's picks again focus on small, overlooked companies: under $3B in market value, followed by few Wall Street analysts, beaten down from their highs, and still profitable at their core. The idea is to be contrarian where it's justified. Small stocks swing harder and can be thinly traded, so keep positions small and use limit orders (an order that only buys at the price you set or lower). Nobody can reliably predict which stocks rise in the next few weeks; these are the best odds over a year or more, not promises. Changes from last week: three new names. Carter's, Shoe Station and Heritage Global come in because they offer good odds on the stress test. Ruger jumped about 13% after strong results on Nov 4, and Build-A-Bear and Travelzoo still offer poor odds, so all three move to the radar. Innoviva stays first: its stock fell 10% to about $19 after a messy quarter, which makes it cheaper. New this week: each pick has a scorecard (red flags, moat, management, price vs similar companies) and a fold-out "Full research notes" section. This week I also made the stress test tougher for four companies whose profits are falling faster than their sales show: Heritage Global, Intermex, Western Union and Sylvamo.

1. Innoviva (INVA) · ~$19, estimated value ~$45, if no growth ~$28, if it goes badly ~$12.50

A $1.4B company that collects royalties on GSK lung inhalers and sells hospital antibiotics. Only five analysts follow it, and even with zero growth its cash earnings look worth about 45% more than today's price.

Scorecard: Red flags 4/10 · Moat 3/5 (Stable) · Management 3/5 · Valuation vs peers: cheap (vs Royalty Pharma, Ligand, Harmony Biosciences)

Latest news: Q3 results came out Nov 4. Revenue was $106M, down 2% and a bit below forecasts. US hospital-drug sales jumped 45% to $43M, and operating profit rose 22%. The headline loss of $1.03 a share came from its stakes in other drug companies falling in value (mainly Armata), not from the business. It bought back $26M of its own shares in the quarter and repeated its goal of at least $150M of US drug sales this year.

Why it sells so low: About half its income is GSK royalties, and the patents behind them are expected to run out around 2031, so investors mark it down years early. Goldman Sachs rates it a Sell with an $18 target. Its profits look messy because the value of its stakes in other companies swings every quarter. If the royalties fade and hospital drugs don't fill the gap, the scan's estimate is too high, which is why the stress test already assumes a 50% cut.

How they plan to grow: Use the steady royalties (about $58M a quarter) to build a hospital-drugs business: Giapreza, Xacduro, Xerava, and Nuzolvence, a newly launched pill for drug-resistant gonorrhea. Analysts expect about $1.91 of adjusted profit per share this year; their average price target is about $35.

Pros: It has $581M of cash, more than enough to cover its $259M of debt due in 2028, and it keeps buying back shares. Hospital drug sales are growing fast (up 45%).

Cons: Its biggest income source has a known end date. Its stakes in other drug companies, like Armata, keep causing big paper losses. Total revenue shrank slightly this quarter.

Is it worth it? If the royalties fade as feared and nothing replaces them, the stress test puts it at about $12.50, a third below today's price. But the possible gain (to about $45) is about $3.90 for every $1 you could lose. Good odds. The odds stay good below about $23.

What to watch: Full-year results around late February 2027, the first full year of Nuzolvence sales, and whether buybacks continue. Main risk: hospital drugs grow too slowly to replace the royalties before 2031.

2. Carter's (CRI) · ~$32, estimated value ~$41, if no growth ~$41, if it goes badly ~$28

America's best-known baby and kids' clothing brand (Carter's, OshKosh), valued at only about $1.2B and down about 28% from its high. It looks cheap even if it never grows.

Scorecard: Red flags 4/10 · Moat 3/5 (Stable) · Management 3/5 · Valuation vs peers: cheap (vs Gap, Levi Strauss, Children's Place)

Latest news: On Oct 30, Q3 profit rose about 40% to $1.03 a share, well above the $0.85 analysts expected, on sales of $752M (flat). Cost cuts, higher prices and about 30 store closures helped. But it lowered its holiday-quarter forecast: sales are expected to fall a few percent, and profit to drop to $1.37–1.55 a share from $1.90 last year.

Why it sells so low: Most of its clothes are made in Asia, so tariffs hit hard, and this year's profits were helped by about $130M of one-time tariff refunds. Fewer babies are born each year in the US, and big stores like Walmart and Amazon compete on price. Investors worry the brand is slowly losing ground, so the scan's estimate could be too high if sales keep slipping.

How they plan to grow: A brand relaunch with more marketing (up about $20M), new areas like older kids, higher-income shoppers and overseas sales, and closing weak stores. Spring orders from retailers are up about 30%, and a new product chief from H&M joins in early 2027.

Pros: More than $525M of cash and strong cash flow ($240–250M expected this year). A brand parents know, and profits are rising thanks to cost cuts.

Cons: Sales aren't growing, and the holiday quarter will be weaker. Tariffs and cotton prices (up about 20%) will raise costs. Part of this year's profit came from one-time tariff refunds.

Is it worth it? If sales slip the way they did in its worst three-year stretch, it's worth about $28, only about 12% below today. The possible gain (to about $41) is about $2.30 for every $1 you could lose. Good odds, as long as you buy below about $32.40, which is close to today's price.

What to watch: Holiday sales in November and December, then full-year results around late February 2027 with the 2027 forecast. Main risk: a promotional holiday season and tariffs squeeze profits again.

  • Cash flow is healthy: $72M of free cash flow so far this year vs about −$180M last year, and inventory is down 11% (a good sign). No auditor issues found.

  • Brand 4, network effects 1, switching costs 1, cost advantage 3, scale 3, intellectual property 2, distribution 4 (its own stores plus Walmart, Target, Amazon), regulatory 1, data 2, customer loyalty 3.

Management (3/5)

  • A CEO who joined in 2025 is relaunching the brand and cutting costs, and the Q3 beat was solid. Insiders own about 5%. Too early to judge the long-term record.

Valuation vs peers

  • By this scan's numbers, Carter's trades at about 8.7 times operating profit (counting debt and cash), Gap at about 5.4 times and Levi Strauss at about 13.8 times. Carter's earns about 11% of its value each year in cash, more than both. Verdict: cheap vs Levi, about fair vs Gap. The discount comes from flat sales.

Earnings call takeaways (Oct 30)

  • Profit beat; September sales grew by double digits after the relaunch; tone upbeat on the brand but cautious on the holidays. Analysts asked why the holiday forecast was cut and whether tariffs are really behind it ("not out of the woods").

3. Shoe Station Group (SHOE) · ~$14.50, estimated value ~$23.50, if no growth ~$23.50, if it goes badly ~$11.50

The family-shoe chain formerly called Shoe Carnival, worth about $395M with no debt and about $132M of cash. Only one analyst follows it, and it's down about a third from its high.

Scorecard: Red flags 5/10 · Moat 2/5 (Stable) · Management 3/5 · Valuation vs peers: cheap (vs Boot Barn, Designer Brands, Genesco)

Latest news: No new results since Sept 10, when Q2 profit fell to $0.23 a share from $0.70 as it cut prices to clear old stock. It lowered this year's forecast to $0.75–0.90 of adjusted profit per share (versus $1.90 last year). Online sales rose 19% while store sales fell, and August sales improved. It raised its dividend to $0.17 a quarter.

Why it sells so low: Profits have more than halved this year, the former CEO left, and an interim CEO is running the company. Its switch to the new Shoe Station name is paused for the rest of the year. Investors worry the store chain is losing out to online sellers and to Nike and others selling direct. On this year's profit, the stock is not that cheap, which is why the stress test already assumes a 50% cut.

How they plan to grow: Clean out old inventory (about $50M less by year-end), lean on boots and winter shoes for the holiday quarter, grow online sales, and resume converting stores to the bigger Shoe Station format once the new leadership is settled.

Pros: No debt, cash covers about a third of its value, and the founding family owns about a third of the shares. It pays a rising dividend and buys back shares.

Cons: Profits more than halved this year, and margins (the profit kept from each sale) aren't expected to recover soon. No permanent CEO yet. Store traffic keeps falling.

Is it worth it? If profits stay at about half their old level, the stress test puts it at about $11.50, about 20% below today. The possible gain (to about $23.50, if profits return toward last year's) is about $3 for every $1 you could lose. Good odds below about $15.50.

What to watch: Q3 results, expected in late November or early December, and the holiday boot season. A permanent CEO appointment would be a big signal. Main risk: price cuts become permanent and profits never recover.

4. Heritage Global (HGBL) · ~$1.25, estimated value ~$2.30, if no growth ~$1.85, if it goes badly ~$1.10

A tiny ($43M) firm that runs auctions of used factory equipment and helps banks sell bad loans. Four analysts follow it, and insiders have been buying shares.

Scorecard: Red flags 6/10 · Moat 2/5 (Stable) · Management 3/5 · Valuation vs peers: cheap (vs Liquidity Services, RB Global, Copart)

Latest news: On Nov 5, Q3 revenue rose 28% to $14.6M and profit was $1.0M ($0.03 a share), up from $0.6M. The equipment-auction side did well. But cash fell to $15.6M from $20.5M at the start of the year as it invested in two loan-trading businesses (DebtX and Boston Note), and DebtX had a "challenging quarter." Earlier this year it shut its lending arm and wrote off about $18M.

Why it sells so low: It's very small and thinly traded, so most funds can't buy it. Its yearly profit has fallen from $15.5M in 2022 to $3.6M in 2025. The lending arm it closed lost money, and the new loan-trading bets are unproven. Because profits have fallen so much more than sales, I made its stress test tougher this week (a 45% cut).

How they plan to grow: Focus on its auction business, which is getting bigger jobs, plus loan-trading through DebtX and Boston Note and joint ventures like its new $2M stake in two Colorado drug factories bought to resell.

Pros: Even under the tougher stress test, the price is only a little above its bad-case value. Insiders are buying, and the core auction business is growing again.

Cons: It's tiny, and few shares trade each day, so prices can jump. Cash is shrinking as it invests, and the lending exit cost about $18M. Profits are a fraction of what they were in 2022.

Is it worth it? In the bad case, profits stay well below their old level and it's worth about $1.10, about 13% below today. The possible gain (to about $2.30) is about $6.50 for every $1 you could lose. Good odds below about $1.50. Keep this one very small: the odds look good on paper, but the company is tiny and its new ventures are unproven.

What to watch: Full-year results around March 2027, the next DebtX results, and whether insiders keep buying. Main risk: the new loan-trading investments keep losing money and burning cash.

5. Smith & Wesson (SWBI) · ~$14.50, estimated value ~$25, if no growth ~$19, if it goes badly ~$8.50

A $650M gun maker with a famous brand, followed by only two analysts. It's still down about 18% from its high, and even with no growth it looks worth about 30% more than today's price.

Scorecard: Red flags 5/10 · Moat 3/5 (Stable) · Management 3/5 · Valuation vs peers: fair (vs Sturm Ruger, American Outdoor Brands, Beretta)

Latest news: The stock rose about 10% this week after rival Ruger reported sales up 17% on Nov 4, a sign gun demand is recovering. Smith & Wesson's own last results (Sept 3): sales up 32% to $113M and a small profit of $0.06 a share, helped by $2.9M of one-time tariff refunds. It expects this quarter's sales to be about 10% higher than last year's.

Why it sells so low: Gun sales have slumped since the pandemic buying spree, profits are thin, and last quarter's improvement came partly from one-time refunds. Cash fell to $19M while borrowing rose to $39M as it built up inventory, so investors doubt the recovery will last.

How they plan to grow: New products (about 38% of sales last year), plus faster-growing areas like gun silencers, hunting rifles, police and overseas sales. Management expects full-year sales growth of 5–7%.

Pros: A famous brand that looks cheap even with no growth. Sales are rising again, and Ruger's strong quarter suggests the whole industry is recovering.

Cons: Profits are tiny and easily wiped out. Borrowing went up and cash went down last quarter. Gun demand rises and falls with politics and headlines.

Is it worth it? If gun sales slump again the way they did after the pandemic boom, it's worth about $8.50. The possible gain (to about $25) is about $1.70 for every $1 at risk. Fair odds: the odds turn good below about $13.90, just under today's price. This week's jump used up some of the bargain.

What to watch: Q2 results, expected in early December, and monthly gun background-check numbers through the holidays. Main risk: thin profits and rising debt if sales stall.
