Each value now uses realistic growth: the lowest of the company's 10-year history, its last 3 years, and what analysts forecast. "If no growth" shows what the business is worth if growth stops completely; buying below that means any future growth comes free. Nobody can reliably predict which stocks rise in the next few weeks, so treat these as the best odds over a year or more, not promises.

Changed after the fix: PayPal (value ~$72 vs $56), Sprouts and EPAM moved to the watchlist. They're still decent, just not cheap enough once you use analysts' growth forecasts instead of their past boom years.

1. Innoviva (INVA) · ~$21, estimated value ~$61, if no growth ~$33

Even if it never grows again, the cash it makes is worth more than 1.5x today's price.

Latest news: Q2 revenue rose 19% to $120M, and hospital drug sales were up 46%. The reported loss came from investments it holds dropping in value, not from the business.

How they plan to grow: Expects over $1B of royalties from GSK lung inhalers over the next five years, mostly from outside the US. It uses that cash to grow its hospital drugs (over $200M a year, growing 40–50%) and to launch zoliflodacin, a new pill for drug-resistant gonorrhea. Analysts expect about 9% revenue growth.

What to watch: Q3 results in early November and the zoliflodacin launch. Risk: royalties fade as the inhalers age.

2. Columbia Sportswear (COLM) · ~$58, estimated value ~$95, if no growth ~$82

A family-run outdoor clothing company (Columbia, prAna, Mountain Hardwear, Sorel) with lots of cash, little debt, and a long record of profits. It trades well below even its no-growth value.

Latest news: Q2 sales rose 2%, beating estimates, and it raised its full-year profit forecast to $4.45–4.90 per share, partly helped by a tariff refund. US sales fell 4%, but international sales grew.

How they plan to grow: Its "ACCELERATE" plan focuses on younger, more active customers, Columbia footwear, and international markets. It bought back $150M of its own stock in Q1. Sales are guided to grow only 1–3% this year.

What to watch: Q3 earnings in late October; management expects $1.15–1.35 per share. Risks: weak US consumers, tariffs, and Sorel boots falling 14%.

3. H&R Block (HRB) · ~$46, estimated value ~$79, if no growth ~$56

America's best-known tax preparer: a steady, cash-rich business people need every year, priced at about 7.5 times next year's expected profit.

Latest news: Fiscal 2026 revenue grew 5% to $3.95B and profit per share grew 14%. It returned $714M to shareholders, raised the dividend 10% (about a 4% yield), and plans $400M more in buybacks.

How they plan to grow: AI tools that help its tax pros and do-it-yourself customers, focusing on higher-value clients who keep coming back, and growing Wave, its small-business software, which has grown double digits two years in a row. It guides fiscal 2027 profit to $6.04–6.24 per share, up about 15%.

What to watch: The fear is that AI and free filing shrink the tax-prep business over time. The next real test is the 2027 tax season (Jan–Apr).

4. Lululemon (LULU) · ~$94, estimated value ~$138 (assumes no growth)

A decade as one of retail's best businesses, down about 46% this year. Analysts expect sales to shrink slightly, so the model gives it zero growth, and it still looks cheap.

Latest news: US sales fell five quarters in a row, tariffs cut profit margins, and some products flopped. Profit is guided down about 17% this year, which isn't in the filings yet, so the true no-growth value is somewhat lower than $138.

How they plan to grow: New CEO Heidi O'Neill, who ran product and brand at Nike, started Sept 8 to fix product freshness. Growth is shifting abroad: China (still about +20%), Mexico (30+ stores planned), and six new countries in 2026.

What to watch: O'Neill's first strategy update and December earnings. Risk: the brand keeps fading in the US.

5. Bath & Body Works (BBWI) · ~$19, estimated value ~$37 (assumes no growth)

Still very profitable with strong cash flow, priced at about 7 times this year's expected earnings. The catch is that sales are slowly shrinking.

Latest news: Q1 beat estimates, but sales fell 3%. Full-year sales are guided down 2.5–4.5%, with earnings of $2.40–2.65 per share. The CFO left in June.

How they plan to grow: CEO Daniel Heaf's "Consumer First" plan: more new products in its core candles and body care, dropping weaker categories (hair, men's grooming), and reaching new customers through Amazon and college bookstores.

What to watch: The holiday season (most of its year) and Q3 earnings in late November. Risk: if sales keep shrinking, cheap stays cheap.
