This week's picks again focus on small, overlooked companies: under $3B in market value, followed by few Wall Street analysts, beaten down from their highs, and still profitable at their core. The idea is to be contrarian where it's justified. Small stocks swing harder and can be thinly traded, so keep positions small and use limit orders. Nobody can reliably predict which stocks rise in the next few weeks; these are the best odds over a year or more, not promises. Changes from last week: Smith & Wesson moves up into the top five, because it's still clearly cheap and its sales are growing again. Buckle drops to the radar list, since its price now roughly equals its estimated value. The next two weeks are busy with results: Travelzoo reports on Oct 27, and Innoviva and Ruger report in early November.

1. Innoviva (INVA) · ~$21, estimated value ~$49, if no growth ~$27

A $1.5B royalty-and-drugs company that only five analysts follow. Even if it never grows, the cash it earns is worth more than today's price.

Latest news: Quiet week. On Oct 19 it said it will present data on its infection drugs at the IDWeek medical meeting. In Q2, revenue rose 19% to $120M and hospital drug sales rose 46%. The reported loss came from its stock holdings in other companies falling in value, not from the business itself.

How they plan to grow: It uses steady royalties from GSK lung inhalers (about $60M a quarter) to build a hospital-drugs business, with at least $150M of US sales expected this year. It also has a newly approved pill for drug-resistant gonorrhea (Nuzolvence). Analysts expect about 9% revenue growth.

What to watch: Q3 results, expected in late October or early November (no date announced yet), and early sales of the new pill. Risk: the royalties fade as the inhalers age, and swings in its investments keep making profits look messy.

2. Build-A-Bear Workshop (BBW) · ~$24, estimated value ~$34, if no growth ~$33

The classic contrarian setup. It's down about two-thirds from its high after a forecast cut, yet it still expects $60–68M of pre-tax profit this year on a market value of about $290M. Only three analysts cover it.

Latest news: No new headlines; the stock slipped about 5% this week. In late August it cut its 2026 revenue forecast to $500–525M after Q2 sales fell 7% and online sales fell 16%. It has kept paying its dividend.

How they plan to grow: More stores and partner-run locations abroad, wholesale deals that put its products in other retailers, and big "experience" stores like its new three-story flagship at ICON Park in Orlando. It returned $23M to shareholders in the first half.

What to watch: The Orlando flagship opens in November, Q3 results come in early December, and then the holiday season makes or breaks the year. Risk: spending on kids stays weak and online sales keep falling.

3. Sturm, Ruger (RGR) · ~$45, estimated value ~$55, if no growth ~$54

A debt-free US gun maker with a long record of profits, followed by only two analysts. Its CEO and directors bought shares with their own money this year.

Latest news: This is a special situation. Italian gunmaker Beretta's offer to buy shares at $44.80 closed on Oct 15, leaving Beretta with about 25% of Ruger. The stock has held up since, trading slightly above the offer price.

How they plan to grow: New products and cost cuts while gun demand stays well below its pandemic peak. With a 25% owner that knows the industry, a full takeover later is possible, but nothing has been offered.

What to watch: Q3 results in early November and any next move by Beretta, such as board seats or a full bid. Risk: without the offer to lean on, the stock depends on gun sales, which are still sliding.

4. Smith & Wesson (SWBI) · ~$14, estimated value ~$25, if no growth ~$19

A $610M gun maker that only two analysts follow. It's down about 22% from its high, yet even with no growth it looks worth about a third more than today's price.

Latest news: In its latest quarter (reported Sept 3), sales jumped 32% to $113M and it swung back to a small profit of $2.6M. Part of the better margin came from one-time tariff refunds, so the true improvement is smaller than it looks.

How they plan to grow: New products (they made up 38% of sales last year), plus faster-growing areas like gun silencers, hunting rifles, police and overseas sales. Management expects this quarter's sales to be about 10% above last year and full-year growth of 5–7%.

What to watch: Q2 results in early December, where management has promised a much better quarter than last year. Risk: profits are still thin, and owning both Ruger and Smith & Wesson doubles your bet on gun demand.

5. Travelzoo (TZOO) · ~$5, estimated value ~$6 (assumes no growth)

A $52M travel-deals company whose founder's family owns about 36%. The stock is down about 59% since it switched to paid memberships, which cost money now but should bring steady fees later.

Latest news: Q2 revenue fell 3% and it lost $2.1M, which it blamed on world conflicts hurting travel. Membership renewals hit a record, and fees collected in advance rose to $13.4M from $8.7M at the start of the year.

How they plan to grow: Building recurring membership fees on top of its advertising business. Management expects revenue to grow again from Q3 onward.

What to watch: Q3 results come out before the market opens on Tuesday, Oct 27, with a call at 11am ET. The key question is whether revenue actually grew again; expect a big move either way. This is the highest-risk pick: it's tiny, losing money, and has only about $8M of cash.

Also on the radar: Buckle (BKE, debt-free with insiders owning about 38%, but now fairly priced; October sales come out Nov 5). Carter's (CRI, the baby-clothes maker, cheap even with no growth and down 27%; it reports Q3 on Monday, Oct 26, but its sales have been shrinking for years). Heritage Global (HGBL, a $43M auction firm whose insiders keep buying, but it took a $16M loss in Q2 while shutting down its lending arm). Left out on purpose: Sylvamo (SLVM) and Western Union (WU) score as very cheap, but office paper and old-style money transfers are shrinking for good. Shoe Station (SHOE) is cheap, but its profits roughly halved and an interim CEO is still resetting its strategy.
