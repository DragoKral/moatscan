This week's picks again focus on small, overlooked companies: under $3B in market value, followed by few Wall Street analysts, beaten down from their highs, and still profitable at their core. The idea is to be contrarian where it's justified. Small stocks swing harder and can be thinly traded, so keep positions small and use limit orders (an order that only buys at the price you set or lower). Nobody can reliably predict which stocks rise in the next few weeks; these are the best odds over a year or more, not promises. Changes from last week: Gentex comes in as the one larger company. It is a debt-free, high-margin car-parts maker that hit a one-year low. Carter's drops to the radar: I made its stress test tougher because its profits fell about 70% from 2023 to 2025, and this year's profits are padded by one-time tariff refunds. That leaves it with poor odds. I also added a tougher stress test for Gentex. Western Union walked away from buying Intermex on Nov 10, and Intermex is fighting it (see the radar). Innoviva, Shoe Station, Heritage Global and Smith & Wesson stay. Smith & Wesson also bought rival Savage Arms in October.

1. Innoviva (INVA) · ~$19, estimated value ~$45, if no growth ~$28, if it goes badly ~$12.50

A $1.4B company that collects royalties on GSK lung inhalers and sells hospital antibiotics. Only five analysts follow it, and even with zero growth its cash earnings look worth about 45% more than today's price.

Scorecard: Red flags 4/10 · Moat 3/5 (Stable) · Management 3/5 · Valuation vs peers: cheap (vs Royalty Pharma, Ligand, Harmony Biosciences)

Latest news: Q3 results came out Nov 4. Revenue was $106M, down 2% and a bit below forecasts. US hospital-drug sales jumped 45% to $43M, and operating profit rose 22%. The headline loss of $1.03 a share came from its stakes in other drug companies falling in value (mainly Armata), not from the business. It bought back $26M of its own shares in the quarter and repeated its goal of at least $150M of US drug sales this year. No major company news since then; the stock has held at about $19.

Why it sells so low: About half its income is GSK royalties, and the patents behind them are expected to run out around 2031, so investors mark it down years early. Goldman Sachs rates it a Sell with an $18 target. Its profits look messy because the value of its stakes in other companies swings every quarter. If the royalties fade and hospital drugs don't fill the gap, the scan's estimate is too high, which is why the stress test already assumes a 50% cut.

How they plan to grow: Use the steady royalties (about $58M a quarter) to build a hospital-drugs business: Giapreza, Xacduro, Xerava, and Nuzolvence, a newly launched pill for drug-resistant gonorrhea. Analysts expect about $1.91 of adjusted profit per share this year; their average price target is about $35.

Pros: It has $581M of cash, more than enough to cover its $259M of debt due in 2028, and it keeps buying back shares. Hospital drug sales are growing fast (up 45%).

Cons: Its biggest income source has a known end date. Its stakes in other drug companies, like Armata, keep causing big paper losses. Total revenue shrank slightly this quarter.

Is it worth it? If the royalties fade as feared and nothing replaces them, the stress test puts it at about $12.50, a third below today's price. But the possible gain (to about $45) is about $3.90 for every $1 you could lose. Good odds. The odds stay good below about $23.

What to watch: Full-year results around late February 2027, the first full year of Nuzolvence sales, and whether buybacks continue. Main risk: hospital drugs grow too slowly to replace the royalties before 2031.

2. Shoe Station Group (SHOE) · ~$14.40, estimated value ~$23.50, if no growth ~$23.50, if it goes badly ~$11.50

The family-shoe chain formerly called Shoe Carnival, worth about $395M with no debt and about $132M of cash. Only one analyst follows it, and it's down about a third from its high.

Scorecard: Red flags 5/10 · Moat 2/5 (Stable) · Management 3/5 · Valuation vs peers: cheap (vs Boot Barn, Designer Brands, Genesco)

Latest news: On Nov 10 the company confirmed it will report Q3 results on Nov 24. The last results were on Sept 10, when Q2 profit fell to $0.23 a share from $0.70 as it cut prices to clear old stock. It lowered this year's forecast to $0.75–0.90 of adjusted profit per share (versus $1.90 last year). Online sales rose 19% while store sales fell, and August sales improved. It raised its dividend to $0.17 a quarter.

Why it sells so low: Profits have more than halved this year, the former CEO left, and an interim CEO is running the company. Its switch to the new Shoe Station name is paused for the rest of the year. Investors worry the store chain is losing out to online sellers and to Nike and others selling direct. On this year's profit, the stock is not that cheap, which is why the stress test already assumes a 50% cut.

How they plan to grow: Clean out old inventory (about $50M less by year-end), lean on boots and winter shoes for the holiday quarter, grow online sales, and resume converting stores to the bigger Shoe Station format once the new leadership is settled.

Pros: No debt, cash covers about a third of its value, and the founding family owns about a third of the shares. It pays a rising dividend and buys back shares.

Cons: Profits more than halved this year, and margins (the profit kept from each sale) aren't expected to recover soon. No permanent CEO yet. Store traffic keeps falling.

Is it worth it? If profits stay at about half their old level, the stress test puts it at about $11.50, about 20% below today. The possible gain (to about $23.50, if profits return toward last year's) is about $3 for every $1 you could lose. Good odds below about $15.50.

What to watch: Q3 results on Nov 24 (confirmed) and the holiday boot season. A permanent CEO appointment would be a big signal. Main risk: price cuts become permanent and profits never recover.

3. Heritage Global (HGBL) · ~$1.15, estimated value ~$2.30, if no growth ~$1.85, if it goes badly ~$1.10

A tiny ($43M) firm that runs auctions of used factory equipment and helps banks sell bad loans. Four analysts follow it, and insiders have been buying shares.

Scorecard: Red flags 6/10 · Moat 2/5 (Stable) · Management 3/5 · Valuation vs peers: cheap (vs Liquidity Services, RB Global, Copart)

Latest news: On Nov 5, Q3 revenue rose 28% to $14.6M and profit was $1.0M ($0.03 a share), up from $0.6M. The equipment-auction side did well. But cash fell to $15.6M from $20.5M at the start of the year as it invested in two loan-trading businesses (DebtX and Boston Note), and DebtX had a "challenging quarter." Earlier this year it shut its lending arm and wrote off about $18M. The stock slipped about 9% this week to around $1.14, with no new company news I could find.

Why it sells so low: It's very small and thinly traded, so most funds can't buy it. Its yearly profit has fallen from $15.5M in 2022 to $3.6M in 2025. The lending arm it closed lost money, and the new loan-trading bets are unproven. Because profits have fallen so much more than sales, I made its stress test tougher this week (a 45% cut).

How they plan to grow: Focus on its auction business, which is getting bigger jobs, plus loan-trading through DebtX and Boston Note and joint ventures like its new $2M stake in two Colorado drug factories bought to resell.

Pros: Even under the tougher stress test, the price is only a little above its bad-case value. Insiders are buying, and the core auction business is growing again.

Cons: It's tiny, and few shares trade each day, so prices can jump. Cash is shrinking as it invests, and the lending exit cost about $18M. Profits are a fraction of what they were in 2022.

Is it worth it? In the bad case, profits stay well below their old level and it's worth about $1.09, only about 5% below today. Because the price sits barely above that, the possible gain (to about $2.30) works out to about $22 for every $1 you could lose on paper. Good odds below about $1.50. Keep this one very small: the odds look good on paper, but the company is tiny and its new ventures are unproven.

What to watch: Full-year results around March 2027, the next DebtX results, and whether insiders keep buying. Main risk: the new loan-trading investments keep losing money and burning cash.

4. Gentex (GNTX) · ~$19, estimated value ~$31, if no growth ~$21.50, if it goes badly ~$12

Gentex makes most of the world's self-dimming car mirrors, has no debt, and keeps far more of each sale as profit than other car-parts makers. At about $3.9B it is bigger and better known than our usual picks (about a dozen analysts, mostly "Hold"), but it hit a one-year low on Nov 9. It is this week's one larger-company exception.

Scorecard: Red flags 4/10 · Moat 4/5 (Stable) · Management 4/5 · Valuation vs peers: cheap for its quality (vs Magna, Lear, Aptiv, Visteon)

Latest news: On Oct 23, Q3 sales were $648M (down 1%) and adjusted profit was $0.47 a share, both below forecasts. Mirror shipments fell 15%, and sales in China fell 44%. It cut this year's sales forecast to about $2.65B and warned of $10–15M of extra costs this quarter that carmakers won't repay until early 2027. It kept its 2027 sales goal of $2.80–2.90B, and the CEO said he is "increasingly confident" about 2027. It bought back 5.7M shares for $133M in the quarter, at about $23.44 each, above today's price.

Why it sells so low: Car production is flat to falling, tariffs and metal costs are squeezing margins (the share of each sale kept as gross profit fell to 33.4% from 34.4%), and China sales are collapsing. Analysts have cut price targets (Goldman Sachs to $27). Long term, some fear cameras could replace mirrors. Its profits fell about 25% in the 2021–22 chip shortage while sales held steady, which the automatic stress test missed, so I added a 25% cut this week.

How they plan to grow: More technology per car: a fourth-generation camera-based "Full Display Mirror", a dimmable sun visor due in late 2027, and driver-monitoring cameras it hopes will reach $80–100M of sales by 2027. Its VOXX premium-audio business (bought in 2025) grew 17% and is now about 15% of sales.

Pros: No debt and about $380M of cash and investments. It has bought back about 5% of its shares this year. It earns profit margins several times higher than its peers'.

Cons: It rises and falls with car production. Shipments of its core product fell 15% last quarter, which is the most serious red flag. It also took $16.9M of write-downs this year and set aside $13.3M more for a loan it made that may not be repaid.

Is it worth it? In the bad case, profits drop by a quarter as they did in 2021–22 and then keep slipping, which makes it worth about $11.90. The possible gain (to about $31) is about $1.60 for every $1 you could lose. Fair odds. The odds turn good below about $18.20, about 5% under today's price.

What to watch: Full-year results and the 2027 outlook, expected in late January 2027, and whether carmakers repay the extra costs in the first half of 2027. Main risk: a car-production slump, or China and Europe program losses that keep shipments falling.

5. Smith & Wesson (SWBI) · ~$15.70, estimated value ~$25, if no growth ~$19, if it goes badly ~$8.50

A gun maker with a famous brand, worth about $700M before its latest deal and followed by only two analysts. Even with no growth it looks worth about 20% more than today's price.

Scorecard: Red flags 5/10 · Moat 3/5 (Stable) · Management 3/5 · Valuation vs peers: fair (vs Sturm Ruger, American Outdoor Brands, Beretta)

Latest news: On Oct 21 it bought Savage Arms, a hunting-rifle maker with about $160M of yearly sales, for $160M: $37.5M in cash plus about 9 million new shares (roughly 20% more shares). The seller, JDH Capital, now owns about 17% of Smith & Wesson and gets a board seat. Management says the deal adds about $0.07 a share to yearly profit. The last quarterly results (Sept 3) showed sales up 32% to $113M and a small profit of $0.06 a share, partly from one-time tariff refunds. The stock is up about 8% this week.

Why it sells so low: Gun sales have slumped since the pandemic buying spree, profits are thin, and last quarter's improvement came partly from one-time refunds. Cash fell to $19M while borrowing rose to $39M as it built up inventory, so investors doubt the recovery will last.

How they plan to grow: Combine with Savage to become the largest US maker of guns for regular buyers, with more hunting rifles. Plus new products (about 38% of sales last year), plus faster-growing areas like gun silencers, hunting rifles, police and overseas sales. Management expects full-year sales growth of 5–7%.

Pros: A famous brand that looks cheap even with no growth. Sales are rising again, and Ruger's strong quarter suggests the whole industry is recovering.

Cons: Profits are tiny and easily wiped out. It just issued about 20% more shares to buy Savage, and borrowing went up last quarter. Gun demand rises and falls with politics and headlines.

Is it worth it? If gun sales slump again the way they did after the pandemic boom, it's worth about $8.50. The possible gain (to about $25) is about $1.30 for every $1 at risk. Fair odds: the odds turn good below about $13.90, about 11% under today's price. Note the scan's numbers don't include the Savage deal yet: the new shares and Savage's profits should roughly cancel out, but it adds uncertainty.

What to watch: Q2 results, expected in early December, and monthly gun background-check numbers through the holidays. Main risk: thin profits and rising debt if sales stall.
